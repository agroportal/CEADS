# Integrated management of a tomato crop

## 1. Production setting

The fictional River Plain Horticulture Cooperative produces fresh-market tomato in two unheated greenhouses and in one open field. Greenhouse Block A contains an indeterminate tomato cultivar trained on vertical strings. Greenhouse Block B contains a cherry tomato cultivar. The open field contains a determinate processing tomato cultivar planted on raised beds. Every tomato block uses drip irrigation, but the irrigation schedule, soil cover, and crop protection programme differ among blocks.

Greenhouse Block A has a sandy loam soil amended with mature compost. Greenhouse Block B uses a peat-free substrate made from composted bark, coconut fibre, and wood fibre. The open-field tomato crop grows on a clay loam soil after a winter cereal. Before transplanting, the field received a cover crop mixture of rye, vetch, and white mustard. The cover crop was terminated mechanically, and most crop residue was left on the soil surface as mulch.

Tomato seedlings were raised in a nursery and transplanted after the risk of frost had passed. The greenhouse tomato plants were grafted onto a vigorous rootstock to improve root development and tolerance to soil-borne disease. The open-field tomato seedlings were not grafted. Plant spacing was adjusted to maintain air circulation, provide access for scouting, and reduce prolonged leaf wetness in the lower canopy.

## 2. Soil fertility and compost management

Soil analysis before planting measured pH, electrical conductivity, soil organic matter, nitrate, ammonium, available phosphorus, exchangeable potassium, calcium, and magnesium. The open field had moderate soil organic matter and sufficient phosphorus, but potassium was uneven across the raised beds. Greenhouse Block A had a low mineral nitrogen concentration after winter, while Greenhouse Block B had adequate nutrients in the fresh substrate.

Mature compost was incorporated into Greenhouse Block A at a moderate rate. The compost supplied stable organic matter and contributed nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients. Fresh manure was avoided because incomplete decomposition could increase salinity, introduce weed seed, and release nitrogen at an unpredictable rate. The compost pile had previously been monitored for temperature, moisture content, aeration, and maturity.

Fertilizer recommendations were calculated from the soil test, expected tomato yield, irrigation water analysis, and nutrient removal. Nitrogen was divided among several fertigation events rather than applied in one large dose. Phosphorus was placed near the root zone at establishment, and potassium supply was increased during fruit development. Calcium availability and irrigation uniformity were monitored to reduce the risk of blossom-end rot.

Excess nitrogen fertilizer can produce a dense tomato canopy that favours whitefly, aphid, grey mould, and late blight. It can also delay fruit maturation and increase nitrate leaching. For that reason, leaf analysis and sap testing were used as supporting evidence rather than as automatic instructions to add fertilizer. The advisory treats fertigation as a controlled agricultural practice, not as a fixed recipe.

## 3. Drip irrigation and water monitoring

All three tomato blocks use pressure-compensating drip irrigation. Filters protect the emitters from sediment, and the irrigation lines are flushed regularly. The fictional farm measures irrigation water pH, electrical conductivity, bicarbonate, nitrate, sodium, and chloride. The open-field system also includes a flow meter so that water use can be compared with the scheduled irrigation volume.

Soil moisture sensors were installed at two depths in the open-field tomato beds. A tensiometer was placed in the main root zone, and a capacitance sensor was placed below the root zone to identify deep percolation. The greenhouse substrate was monitored using container weight and drainage fraction. These measurements were interpreted together with crop growth stage, solar radiation, air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, rainfall, and reference evapotranspiration.

Drip irrigation was scheduled in short applications during fruit enlargement. Irrigation was postponed after effective rainfall in the open field. In the greenhouse, irrigation frequency increased during sunny weather but decreased when cloud cover reduced transpiration. The objective was to maintain stable soil moisture without saturating the root zone.

Uneven soil moisture can contribute to fruit cracking, blossom-end rot, poor nutrient uptake, and irregular tomato size. Excess drip irrigation can move nitrate below the roots, create anaerobic soil conditions, and increase the risk of root disease. Insufficient drip irrigation can cause drought stress, stomatal closure, flower abortion, and reduced tomato yield. The scouting team therefore checked both the irrigation controller and the plants rather than relying on a sensor reading alone.

Fertigation was applied through the drip irrigation network only after the system reached normal operating pressure. Clean water was used to push fertilizer solution out of the lines at the end of each fertigation event. Backflow prevention was maintained between the fertilizer tank and the water source. The operator recorded the fertilizer product, nitrogen concentration, irrigation duration, block, date, and estimated nutrient dose.

## 4. Canopy management and pollination

Greenhouse tomato plants were trained to a single stem. Side shoots were removed when small, and lower leaves were pruned gradually to improve air movement. Pruning tools were disinfected between greenhouse rows. Workers avoided pruning when tomato leaves were wet because wounds and splashed water can facilitate pathogen movement.

The greenhouse vents opened automatically when air temperature and relative humidity exceeded the control range. Horizontal airflow fans reduced pockets of humid air. Condensation on the greenhouse film was directed away from the crop. These measures shortened leaf wetness duration and made conditions less favourable for grey mould and late blight.

Bumblebee colonies supported pollination in the greenhouse tomato crop. Colony activity was checked during flowering, and the use of insecticides harmful to pollinators was avoided. The open-field tomato crop received visits from wild bees, hoverflies, and other flower-visiting insects. Flower abundance in the field margin provided additional food for beneficial insects.

## 5. Monitoring for whitefly and aphid

Yellow sticky traps were placed slightly above the tomato canopy to monitor adult whitefly and winged aphid. Trap counts were recorded by block and week. The team also inspected the underside of tomato leaves for whitefly eggs, nymphs, and adults. Aphid colonies were recorded separately because sticky traps alone do not measure the density of non-winged aphids on plants.

In Greenhouse Block A, a few greenhouse whitefly adults and nymphs were observed on older tomato leaves. Numbers were below the fictional intervention threshold. Parasitized whitefly scales indicated activity by the parasitoid wasp *Encarsia formosa*. The farm released additional parasitoid wasps as a preventive biological control measure. No broad-spectrum insecticide was used.

In Greenhouse Block B, two small aphid colonies were found near a doorway. Lacewing larvae and hoverfly larvae were present in the same area. The affected tomato shoots were marked for follow-up inspection. Excess nitrogen fertigation was avoided because soft vegetative growth can favour aphid population growth.

Whitefly can transmit plant viruses and produce honeydew that supports sooty mould. Aphid can also transmit viruses and distort young growth. Nevertheless, the presence of a pest does not automatically justify pesticide application. Integrated pest management considers pest density, crop stage, virus risk, natural enemies, greenhouse hygiene, and the selectivity of any proposed plant protection product.

The terms whitefly, aphid, parasitoid wasp, lacewing, hoverfly, biological control, integrated pest management, and tomato are repeated here intentionally. They provide a simple way to check whether AgroAnnotator counts multiple surface forms and whether the selected ontology maps a specific organism to a broader pest or beneficial-insect concept.

## 6. Disease prevention: late blight, grey mould, and bacterial spot

The open-field tomato crop was inspected after a period of rainfall and cool nights. No confirmed late blight lesion was found, but the weather station recorded several hours of leaf wetness. Scouts were instructed to look for water-soaked leaf lesions, rapidly expanding brown areas, and pale sporulation on the underside of leaves. Suspected material would be isolated and submitted to a plant health laboratory rather than diagnosed from a photograph alone.

Late blight risk was managed through resistant cultivar selection, healthy transplants, field ventilation, irrigation timing, crop residue management, and frequent scouting. Overhead irrigation was avoided because the farm already had drip irrigation. Volunteer tomato and potato plants were removed because they can maintain a pathogen between crops. Discarded tomato fruit and infected crop residue would not be left beside the production block.

Grey mould was considered a higher risk in dense greenhouse areas with pruning wounds and prolonged relative humidity. Dead leaves, senescent flowers, and damaged fruit were removed from the tomato canopy. Workers placed diseased crop residue in closed containers and cleaned tools before moving to another greenhouse block. Ventilation and careful irrigation reduced condensation.

Bacterial spot prevention focused on clean seed, healthy seedlings, sanitation, and avoidance of splash dispersal. Workers did not handle wet tomato plants. Reusable trays were washed and disinfected. The advisory did not prescribe a specific pesticide because product authorization, resistance management, local regulation, disease confirmation, and crop stage must all be checked by a qualified adviser.

For annotation testing, late blight is mentioned in the introduction, irrigation section, canopy section, and disease section. A merged concept summary should not create a separate concept simply because late blight occurs in different chunks. It should record multiple occurrences when the AgroPortal Annotator recognizes the phrase consistently.

## 7. Soil-borne disease and root health

Tomato roots were inspected where plants showed temporary wilting. Healthy roots were light coloured and distributed through the bed. No widespread root rot was observed. The team considered waterlogging, high electrical conductivity, root-knot nematode, Fusarium wilt, and bacterial wilt as possible causes that would require different diagnostic evidence.

Crop rotation in the open field separates tomato from other solanaceous crops. Cereal and legume phases were included, although crop rotation alone cannot manage every soil-borne pathogen. Grafted tomato plants were used in the greenhouse as one component of root disease management. Steam treatment or soil solarization could be considered for a confirmed local problem, subject to energy use, soil biology, cost, and regulation.

The compost amendment was mature and well aerated. Immature compost was avoided near tomato roots. Irrigation drainage was checked after heavy rainfall because saturated soil can reduce oxygen availability. These root-health observations connect soil, water, compost, plant disease, and crop rotation concepts within the same section.

## 8. Weed management and field margins

The open-field tomato beds were covered with biodegradable mulch, while the alleys carried a mown grass cover. Hand weeding removed weeds emerging through planting holes. Mechanical cultivation was used only when the soil surface was dry enough to avoid compaction. Herbicide drift from neighbouring land was identified as a risk to both tomato plants and field-margin vegetation.

Field margins contained flowering plants that supported pollinators and natural enemies. However, scouts also checked the margins for volunteer tomato, volunteer potato, and invasive weeds. The goal was not to remove all non-crop vegetation but to manage specific hosts and prevent weed seed production.

After harvest, crop residue would be assessed for disease symptoms. Healthy crop residue could enter a managed composting process, while residue with regulated plant disease would be handled according to plant-health instructions. Equipment hygiene would reduce the movement of contaminated soil, weed seed, and plant debris.

## 9. Harvest quality and post-harvest handling

Tomato fruit was harvested at a maturity stage appropriate for the target market. Pickers rejected fruit with severe cracking, sunscald, active decay, or insect damage. Harvest crates were clean, intact, and used only for produce. Tomato fruit was protected from direct sun while waiting for transport.

The packing area separated dirty field containers from clean market containers. Water used for washing met the farm's hygiene requirements, and water temperature was managed to reduce infiltration into fruit. The cooperative recorded harvest date, tomato block, cultivar, lot, destination, and rejected quantity for traceability.

Cooling removed field heat, but storage temperature was selected carefully because tomato can suffer chilling injury. Relative humidity was managed to reduce water loss without promoting condensation. The quality team monitored firmness, colour, soluble solids, physical damage, and decay.

Post-harvest losses were connected to production decisions. Irregular drip irrigation can increase cracking. Excess nitrogen can delay maturation. Poor calcium transport can contribute to blossom-end rot. Whitefly honeydew can contaminate fruit, and late blight can destroy foliage and fruit. These repeated links are intentional test signals for concept occurrence counting.

## 11. Weekly action list

1. Inspect tomato leaves for whitefly, aphid, late blight, grey mould, and bacterial spot.
2. Read soil moisture sensors and compare them with rainfall, evapotranspiration, crop stage, and direct root-zone observation.
3. Check drip irrigation pressure, filter condition, emitter uniformity, drainage, and flow-meter records.
4. Review fertigation records for nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, electrical conductivity, and irrigation duration.
5. Record bumblebee activity and avoid plant protection products that are incompatible with pollinators or biological control agents.
6. Remove diseased crop residue, clean pruning tools, and maintain separation among greenhouse blocks.
7. Map weed patches and volunteer tomato or potato plants before they produce seed or sustain disease.
8. Link harvest defects to the relevant tomato block so that crop management and post-harvest teams can investigate together.

## 12. Example narrative for a second scouting round

Seven days later, the fictional team returned after warm weather. Soil moisture in the open-field tomato beds had declined, and the tensiometer indicated that drip irrigation should resume. Reference evapotranspiration had increased, but a short irrigation cycle was chosen to avoid deep percolation. The operator confirmed normal filter pressure and recorded the water volume.

Whitefly counts increased slightly in Greenhouse Block A. Parasitized whitefly nymphs were still present, so the biological control programme continued. The team released parasitoid wasps and removed heavily infested lower leaves. In Greenhouse Block B, aphid colonies had not expanded, and lacewing larvae remained active. No broad-spectrum insecticide was applied.

One suspicious tomato leaflet from the open field was sent to a plant health laboratory. Until a diagnosis was available, the team treated the observation as unconfirmed rather than calling it late blight. Workers avoided moving wet foliage between blocks, removed nearby crop residue, and increased scouting after rainfall. This distinction between a suspected symptom and a confirmed plant disease is important in both agricultural practice and semantic annotation.

Fertigation in the greenhouse supplied nitrogen and potassium in small doses. Leaf analysis did not support an emergency increase in nitrogen fertilizer. Stable soil moisture and balanced nutrition remained priorities for tomato fruit quality. Harvest records showed fewer cracked fruit in rows where drip irrigation uniformity had been corrected.

## 13. Interpretation cautions

An ontology annotator identifies concepts mentioned in text; it does not verify that an agronomic diagnosis or recommendation is correct. A match for late blight means that the document contains a phrase associated with a concept, not that the fictional tomato crop has late blight. Likewise, a match for insecticide does not mean that an insecticide was applied or recommended. Negation, uncertainty, timing, and responsibility remain important for downstream interpretation.

The document contains specific organisms as well as broader categories. Depending on the ontology and Annotator settings, whitefly may be linked to a taxon, a pest concept, or both. Tomato may be linked to the plant, the crop, or the harvested product. Drip irrigation may be linked to an irrigation method, while fertigation may be represented as fertilizer application through irrigation. Results should therefore be evaluated at both the surface-form and concept-URI levels.
